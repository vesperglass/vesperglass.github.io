## The light that can be carried

Among the bright seams found on remote stellar bodies, there is sometimes a crystal that seems to hold a little of the sky within it. The travelers of Vesperglass call these fragments **starshards**. They are gathered intact and brought home in padded, dark-glass cases; a shard is not a lamp, and it gives no useful light until it is worked.

The oldest Foundry account describes a starshard as “a piece of night remembering the shape of day.” Its blue-black faces catch the color of the star that formed it. When turned, a thin gold line appears beneath the surface: a trace that the shard can be coaxed to release.

### The shard's three signs

<div class="shard-signs">
  <div><span class="sign-number">01</span><strong>Memory planes</strong><p>Fine internal layers split and return starlight. Foundry hands read their direction before grinding.</p></div>
  <div><span class="sign-number">02</span><strong>Bright seam</strong><p>A narrow inner glimmer; its hue hints at the star under which the crystal formed.</p></div>
  <div><span class="sign-number">03</span><strong>Fracture face</strong><p>The outer facets are scored and sorted. Clean fractures make a steadier, finer dust.</p></div>
</div>

### Cabinet records · interpretive specimens

These proposed cabinet notes group three forms by their visible traits. The surviving accounts do not establish a formal scientific classification.

<dl class="folio-glossary">
  <div><dt>Blue seam</dt><dd>A compact shard with one clear inner line. A useful teaching specimen for showing how a bright seam can hint at the star beneath which a crystal formed.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>Split heart</dt><dd>A larger fragment whose memory planes divide around a central fracture. Foundry hands might sort it for careful grinding rather than treat the split as a defect.</dd></div>
  <div><dt>Quiet glass</dt><dd>A dark, low-glimmer shard whose light appears only when turned. Its cabinet note would record the angle and conditions of observation before assigning any meaning to its color.</dd></div>
</dl>

### Case record · proposed receiving label

Starshards are said to travel intact in padded, dark-glass cases. The account gives no opening, inspection, or storage procedure. #### Reading the label

The first two fields summarize the account’s stated material and transit. They establish that the shard is carried intact in a padded, dark-glass case; they do not tell us how the case is opened, whether it is sealed, how it is oriented, or what happens at receipt. The remaining fields are proposed blanks for a cataloguer to preserve those absences. They are not evidence that a particular case arrived, that a receiver inspected it, or that the Foundry uses this form.

| Label field | What the account supports | What remains open |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Material | The fragments are called starshards. | A particular fragment’s form, hue, or classification. |
| Transit case | Padded, dark glass; the shard is brought home intact. | Construction, closure, dimensions, and any handling instructions. |
| Receipt | No receiving event is described. | Date, origin, case condition, contents examination, and receiver. |
| Cabinet placement | The folio offers proposed cabinet notes as examples. | Whether a real specimen was catalogued or placed in a cabinet. |

This distinction matters because the three cabinet forms above—*Blue seam*, *Split heart*, and *Quiet glass*—are proposed interpretive notes, not a classification recorded by the Foundry. A label can refer to one only when its proposed status stays attached. The case description by itself does not establish that any of those examples was carried, received, or examined.

For the wider material account, see [Tideglass Foundry]({{ '/library/tideglass-foundry/' | relative_url }}), where the recorded sequence is cleaning, grinding between mirrored plates, sifting, and tempering. That process begins after the starshard reaches Vesperglass; neither folio supplies a procedure for opening or moving its transit case.

### Field plate · the Blue-Sill Basin

The Blue-Sill Basin field account describes a shallow depression where a dark ridge crosses a pale mineral shelf. No surviving survey identifies its coordinates or confirms a starshard deposit there. The place name and terrain description are preserved as an interpretive account.

Fine blue seams catch the low light along the ridge's sheltered side. The account notes scattered glints but gives no confirmed deposit, coordinates, or survey result.

<aside class="field-note"><span class="field-note-mark" aria-hidden="true">✳</span><p><strong>Survey note · interpretation</strong><br>No coordinates or confirmed deposit accompany the Blue-Sill Basin account. Its terrain is an interpretation, and its connection to starshards remains unverified.</p></aside>

## How a shard becomes stardust

Raw starshards travel first to the **Tideglass Foundry** at Vesperglass. There, the shards are cleaned of their host stone, ground between mirrored plates, and sifted until only a fine, even powder remains. The last step is called *tempering*: the dust is exposed to the Gate's low, singing resonance until its scattered glints settle into one quiet color.

The finished material is **stardust**. In a sealed vessel it looks almost still. Tip the vessel and a slow aurora passes through it, as if each grain were trying to remember a direction.

### Material state card · sealed and tipped

This proposed comparison card keeps the account's two descriptions beside one another. It is a reading aid for the language of the folio, not a test result or a vessel-handling instruction.

| Reading in the account | Described appearance | What the description does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vessel sealed | The stardust looks almost still. | Its quantity, packing, exact vessel construction, or whether “still” describes anything beyond its appearance. |
| Vessel tipped | A slow aurora passes through the dust. | How far or quickly the vessel moves, how long the light lasts, or what causes the effect. |

The contrast is a change in what the observer sees: near-stillness in the sealed vessel, then a slow aurora when it is tipped. “Aurora” is the account's image for the passing light, not a measured motion or explanation of its cause. No material, dimensions, closure design, or method of handling the vessel is recorded here. The [Tideglass Foundry folio]({{ '/library/tideglass-foundry/' | relative_url }}) describes how stardust is made; the [Words of Passage]({{ '/library/words-of-passage/' | relative_url }}) cross-reference distinguishes the material from the starwell that carries it.

## A furnace that does not burn

The train's fuel chamber is called the **starwell**. It does not burn stardust as an ordinary furnace burns coal. Instead, the locomotive feeds a measured thread of dust into its glass heart. The grains answer one another, gathering into a single pulse that lets the train lean against distance and find the Gate's far side.

That is why the train is more than a vehicle. It is a moving instrument tuned to the Gate: brass for its frame, dark glass for its heart, and stardust for the note that opens a road. When a train leaves at speed, the portal gathers around it in a bright, expanding bloom. On arrival the bloom opens outward again, and the locomotive emerges already carrying the light of its return journey.

<aside class="field-note"><span class="field-note-mark" aria-hidden="true">✳</span><p><strong>A note from the keepers</strong><br>The bloom is a passage taking shape, not an explosion. The train is never consumed by the light; it is briefly hidden by the road it has made.</p></aside>
