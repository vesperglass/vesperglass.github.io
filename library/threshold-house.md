## The place in the atlas

Threshold House is one of Vesperglass's named floating platforms. Its name suggests a place of arrival, waiting, or transition, but surviving accounts do not describe its purpose or identify its inhabitants.

## An architectural reading

Threshold House is a quiet place to pause before crossing from one part of Vesperglass to another. A long bench leaves the central passage unobstructed, while lamps mark the opening. No dated account confirms these furnishings or a custom associated with them.

## Phrasebook slip · words for arriving

No surviving account records the speech of Threshold House or names a keeper who welcomes arrivals. The three lines below are proposed language for the still-open threshold: examples of how someone might offer rest or conversation without asking for a traveler's destination. They are not quotations or established etiquette.

| Proposed greeting | A moment it might suit | Possible reply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **“You are here; that is enough for now.”** | Someone has arrived but is not ready to recount the journey. The greeting acknowledges their presence without asking for an explanation. | **“Give me a little quiet, then.”** |
| **“Would you like a question, or a quiet?”** | The pause has begun to open into conversation, but the arriving person may still choose not to tell their story. | **“A quiet first; the question can wait.”** |
| **“Shall we leave the crossing unnamed?”** | Two people have different memories of a passage, or neither can say where the road led. The words leave room for uncertainty instead of settling a version. | **“For tonight, let it remain open.”** |

### Place name and event name · what remains named

The proposed line asks whether two people should leave a crossing unnamed. The [Vesperglass Gate]({{ '/library/vesperglass-gate/' | relative_url }}) has an established name in the Encyclopedia and is also the passage described in crossing accounts. These uses answer different questions: *Gate* identifies a place and its role; *a crossing* can refer to one event whose date, destination, and travelers are not supplied.

| Scope | Phrasebook slip | Gate and travel record | Boundary of the comparison |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Subject** | “The crossing” is the passage or event the imagined speakers remember differently. | The Vesperglass Gate is a named platform and the passage between worlds. | The general place-name does not identify the particular event the speakers might have in mind. |
| **Naming** | Leaving a crossing unnamed is a proposed conversational choice in an imagined exchange. | “Vesperglass Gate” remains the recorded place-name; [Words of Passage]({{ '/library/words-of-passage/' | relative_url }}) distinguishes that name from the passage role. | Choosing not to name an event does not rename the Gate or establish a local rule against saying its name. |
| **Identity** | The line gives no speaker, date, or itinerary. | The Gate account describes how passage occurs, but does not attach the scene to a named traveler or dated journey. | Neither text identifies one shared crossing, witness, passenger, or destination. |
| **Purpose** | The suggested reply—“For tonight, let it remain open”—leaves room for unresolved memories. | The travel terms describe the Gate's role, not a duty to narrate a passage. | The private tone of the proposed exchange is not evidence of an official record, hidden route, or required silence. |

The distinction lets a reader keep both ideas in view without forcing them together: the Gate can be named while a particular crossing remains unidentified. This is a reading aid for the phrasebook, not a correction to the Gate account or a new account of traveler etiquette. The [Train Dock docket]({{ '/library/train-dock/' | relative_url }}) likewise leaves event-specific fields blank; it does not connect the proposed conversation to a recorded departure.

### How the slips are meant to work

These lines offer three kinds of welcome: recognition, choice, and room for an unresolved account. The suggested replies are alternatives, not required responses; silence would work just as well. No surviving account establishes the phrases as local custom or assigns a route, time, or history to a particular arrival. They pair with the Gate folio's accounts of an unnamed crossing and the Workshop's proposed practice of carrying an open question forward, while adding no new facts about either place. ## Room-sound note · proposed readings

No surviving account describes how Threshold House sounds. The chamber, bench, lamp niches, and open glass arch belong to a reconstructed interior, so the following acoustic readings are proposals attached to that arrangement. They are written to help readers picture a conversation, not transcribed testimony or evidence of a particular arrival.

| Place in the proposed room | Possible sound | How it might shape a conversation | What remains unknown |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Beneath the tall glass arch | A short phrase might return as a faint second impression from above. | A greeting could feel shared with someone standing farther inside, while its last words remain briefly suspended. | Whether the arch reflects a voice, and its height, curvature, and material. |
| Beside the long bench | A quiet answer might seem close and contained against the side of the chamber. | The bench offers a pause for an arrival who wants to speak softly or wait before answering. | The wall surfaces, distance from the arch, and whether anyone uses the bench. |
| Across the open threshold | A few footfalls might be easier to notice than speech. | The moment of entering could become clear without requiring a greeting or explanation. | The floor material, any echo, and whether a crossing was underway. |

These are three readings of the same reconstructed space, not a catalogue of established sounds. Each one begins with a visible feature in the plan or interior plate and asks what a listener might notice there. The effects are deliberately tentative: a faint return from the arch does not prove a vaulted ceiling, a contained answer does not establish soft furnishings, and a distinct footfall does not identify the floor.

The note pairs naturally with the proposed arrival phrases above. A short greeting at the arch, a quiet reply by the bench, or silence at the threshold are optional scenes, not etiquette or requirements. The Gate folio preserves two contrasting recollections about hush and singing at passage, but those accounts do not verify these chamber sounds or locate them in Threshold House.
