## One sky, more than one map

A constellation is a pattern seen from Earth. The stars that seem to sit together in a figure may be far apart in space; the outline belongs to our point of view. NASA describes the familiar western figure of Orion as the Greek hunter, with three bright stars forming a belt and a sword below. NASA also notes that cultures have grouped and named stars in different ways over time. Orion the hunter is one influential reading of the sky, not a universal one.

That distinction matters. A star pattern can be a useful map without being the only map.

## Rigel and Puanga

Rigel is one of the bright stars in Orion. In the Māori context documented by **Te Ara**, Rigel is called **Puanga**. The encyclopedia records that a number of iwi and hapū in Taiuru—including Taranaki, Whanganui, and Rangitīkei—use Puanga as a marker for the new year. It also describes Puanganui-o-te-rangi, a seasonal grouping that connects Puanga with Puanga Hori (Procyon) and Whakaahu (Gemini).

This is a specific account tied to particular communities and practices. It should not be generalized into a single Māori sky tradition: Te Ara notes that some northern iwi know Rigel as Puangarua, a different name for the same star. The sky here is not merely a picture to recognize. It participates in a calendar and a cycle of ceremonies.

## Pattern, object, relationship

The Greek hunter and Puanga are not two labels for an identical story. One describes a figure traced among stars; the other, in the source above, locates a star within seasonal knowledge and community practice. Modern astronomy adds another view: the apparent closeness of stars on a chart does not mean they are neighbors in space.

Together these views make Orion interesting to study. A constellation is at once a way to find stars, a record of human attention, and a reminder that looking up is always done from somewhere.
